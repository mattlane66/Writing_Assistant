# Semantic Composition Reference

## Governing law

Syntax is the consequence of thought, not evidence that style has been applied.

Build prose in this order:

1. purpose and whole-piece job
2. reality and semantic relations
3. paragraph thought movement
4. information structure
5. syntax
6. literal audit
7. preservation check

Use this reference proportionately. Short, simple writing should not be forced through visible machinery.

## Pass 0: whole-piece frame

Before drafting or substantially revising, determine:

- who the reader is;
- what the piece must accomplish for that reader;
- what the reader should understand, believe, feel, decide, or do afterward, if anything;
- the governing claim, question, tension, event, or request;
- the evidence, facts, observations, or constraints that actually support the piece;
- the order in which the reader needs those things;
- what does not belong.

Sentence craft cannot rescue a piece with the wrong purpose, reader, scope, evidence, or sequence.

## Semantic model before syntax

For any sentence or passage whose meaning is not trivial, identify the relevant underlying relations:

- agent: who or what acts;
- action or state: what actually happens or holds;
- patient or object: what the action affects;
- experiencer: who perceives, feels, remembers, knows, or believes;
- time and aspect: what happens first, during, after, repeatedly, or continuously;
- cause and effect;
- contrast or concession;
- comparison class;
- condition and consequence;
- possession, location, movement, containment, quantity, and scope;
- what is known, inferred, uncertain, or merely possible.

The grammatical subject need not always be the agent. Choose subject position deliberately for emphasis, cohesion, information order, or genre. Never let syntax accidentally assign agency, causality, chronology, or responsibility to the wrong entity.

Prefer a verb when the sentence is fundamentally about an action. A nominalization is legitimate when the action itself genuinely needs to become an object of thought.

## Thought movements

Shape paragraphs and passages by movements of thought rather than by decorative sentence templates. Useful movements include:

- observation -> anomaly -> closer observation -> discovery
- received belief -> contradictory particulars -> revised understanding
- instance -> instance -> instance -> emergent whole
- claim -> counterexample -> qualification
- abstract quantity -> embodied comparison -> felt conclusion
- whole -> detail -> smaller detail -> whole seen differently
- expectation -> violation -> explanation
- past condition -> present consequence -> surviving residue
- intended action -> interruption -> shifted attention -> discovery
- evidence -> evidence -> evidence -> implied conclusion
- proposition -> complication -> narrower, truer proposition
- visible scene -> micro-detail -> invisible process -> transformed return
- apparent explanation -> contradiction -> better explanation

These are possibilities, not templates. Discover the movement supported by the material.

## Information structure

Before choosing sentence shape, decide:

- what the reader already knows;
- what is new;
- what should arrive first;
- what deserves end position;
- what should be delayed;
- what can remain implied;
- what expectation the sentence creates;
- whether the ending should fulfill, redirect, complicate, or intentionally violate that expectation.

Two sentences can represent the same facts while creating different reader experiences because they order the information differently.

## Syntax follows the cognitive job

Choose syntax only after the semantic and informational job is clear.

- Let a sentence be short when its thought benefits from independence.
- Let a sentence accumulate when qualifications, consequences, examples, or perceptions genuinely accumulate.
- Use a fragment when omitted material is already available and the fragment gains useful force, pace, emphasis, or implication.
- Use repetition or parallelism when the underlying ideas are genuinely parallel or when recurrence itself performs the work.
- Use subordination when one idea truly depends on another.
- Use coordination when the ideas deserve comparable grammatical weight.
- Use passive voice when it improves emphasis, cohesion, information order, or appropriately backgrounds an agent.
- Use transitions when they name a real relation the reader might otherwise miss.

Do not vary sentence length or structure merely to create "human" variation. Do not add fragments, em dashes, inversions, appositives, or conspicuous constructions simply to produce style.

A conspicuous construction must do work that a plainer alternative would do less well.

## Generic content is a failure class

Fluent prose can be empty.

Ask:

- Could this sentence fit a different company, person, problem, or essay by swapping a few nouns?
- What does this sentence know that a generic sentence would not?
- Does a claim rest on a concrete fact, observation, distinction, example, mechanism, or source?
- Is the specificity actually supported?

Prefer supported particulars when they add evidence, distinction, or understanding. Do not force specificity for texture. If a needed particular is unknown, preserve the gap, ask when necessary, or use a visible placeholder. Never invent detail to make prose vivid.

Plain is not the same as generic. A plain sentence can be exact.

## Literal audit

Treat the draft as untrusted text. Temporarily disregard what the writer meant and ask what the words and syntax literally assert.

Test only where relevant:

- Who is the grammatical subject, and what does the sentence literally make it do?
- What does each modifier attach to?
- What is the nearest plausible antecedent of each pronoun or relative clause?
- What does "as" or "while" assert is simultaneous?
- What does "because," "due to," or "therefore" assert caused what?
- What does "but," "however," "whereas," "although," or "despite" assert is in opposition?
- What two things are actually being compared?
- Does tense or aspect match the intended chronology?
- Does a word presuppose something the writer did not intend?
- Can a human body or physical object actually perform the described action sequence?
- Does a metaphor assign incompatible literal properties when extended?
- Does a qualifier apply to the intended scope?
- Does one word already entail another, making the added word redundant?
- Has an action been nominalized in a way that hides useful agency or motion?
- Has an abstraction replaced an available concrete fact?
- Has a necessary participant disappeared?
- Does numerical or technical language claim precision the source does not support?
- Does the opening of the sentence create a promise that the ending fails to fulfill?
- Does the sentence explain something the reader can already infer?
- Is a supposedly concrete detail actually present in the source?

Repair the semantic relation before polishing the wording.

## Preservation and over-correction

Editing has three valid outcomes:

1. bad -> repair
2. good -> leave alone
3. over-edited -> restore

No change is an active editorial decision.

Do not assume that editing requires intervention. If the original is already accurate, clear, purposeful, and voice-consistent, preserve it.

### Bad -> repair

Bad:
"The completion of the rollout resulted in an improvement in response time."

Better when the action matters:
"Finishing the rollout improved response time."

The gain is restored action and agency, not simply fewer words.

Bad:
"As the meeting ended, Priya walked to the elevator and had already sent the report."

The chronology is confused. Fix the event order before choosing the sentence shape.

### Good -> leave alone

Good:
"The samples were stored at minus twenty degrees."

If the actor is unknown or irrelevant and the procedure matters, passive voice is doing useful work. Do not invent an actor merely to make the sentence active.

Good:
"No. Not yet."

Fragments can be exact when the omitted material is obvious and the rhythm serves the exchange. Do not expand them merely because they are fragments.

### Over-edited -> restore

Original:
"The train was late. We missed the meeting."

Over-edited:
"Consequently, due to the train's delayed arrival, we were unfortunately unable to attend the meeting."

The revision manufactures formality, redundancy, and connective language. Restore the simpler relation unless additional nuance is required.

Original:
"I knew the room by its hum—the radiator, the fridge, my father pretending not to listen."

Over-edited:
"I recognized the room by the sounds of its appliances and by my father's subtle behavior, which suggested that he was listening."

The revision explains away implication and removes the particulars that carried the voice.

## Voice samples

When the user supplies representative writing samples, treat them as evidence of the writer's prior, not as a bag of surface tics.

Infer, where supported:

- what the writer notices;
- what the writer omits;
- degree of explicitness;
- abstraction versus concreteness;
- how claims are qualified;
- how much inference is left to the reader;
- typical emphasis and information order;
- sentence pressure, cadence, and density;
- humor, distance, and stance.

Do not copy distinctive phrases, repeated punctuation habits, or conspicuous mannerisms merely to mimic the sample. Rules govern judgment; samples help establish the writer's sound.

## Stop rule

Stop when another change offers no clear gain in truth, meaning, reasoning, structure, comprehension, implication, voice, or sentence craft.

Do not improve prose into sameness.
