# Example-derived sentence and paragraph repertoire

## Purpose

This reference preserves the concrete syntactic and compositional possibilities distilled from close reading of strong prose examples during development of Writing Assistant.

The forms below are **not templates, style targets, or mandatory variety devices**. They are named possibilities. Use one only when its semantic, informational, rhetorical, or rhythmic job is already present in the material.

All examples here are newly written synthetic examples. They do not reproduce source passages.

The governing rule remains:

> **Syntax is the consequence of thought, not evidence that style has been applied.**

For every form:

1. identify the underlying relation or movement;
2. decide whether the form makes that relation clearer, stronger, more legible, or more exact;
3. reject the form if it merely makes the prose look literary, varied, or human;
4. preserve a plainer sentence when the conspicuous form adds no material value.

## Sentence-level forms

### appositive-fragment — Appositive fragment

**Job:** Rename, specify, correct, or reframe a preceding noun or proposition after the main sentence has already landed.

**Use when:** the second naming deserves independent emphasis but does not need a new predication.

**Avoid when:** the fragment merely decorates the sentence or repeats what the reader already knows.

**Synthetic example:**  
The team found one remaining dependency. **A billing rule nobody owned.**

### noun-phrase-relative-fragment — Noun phrase with relative-clause fragment

**Job:** Isolate the exact person, object, condition, or constraint that matters, with the relative clause doing the identifying work.

**Use when:** identification or selection is itself the point.

**Avoid when:** the fragment leaves the reader waiting for a predicate that is actually needed.

**Synthetic example:**  
Everything else could move. **The one deadline that could not.**

### temporal-opener-content-clause — Temporal opener plus content clause

**Job:** Establish the time state first, then reveal the proposition whose meaning depends on that time.

**Use when:** chronology changes how the reader interprets what follows.

**Avoid when:** the temporal phrase is throat-clearing or the time does not alter the proposition.

**Synthetic example:**  
**By morning, what looked like delay had become cancellation.**

### proper-noun-fragment — Proper-noun fragment

**Job:** Delay the identity of a person, place, product, or institution until after the role or consequence has been established.

**Use when:** the act of naming is the turn.

**Avoid when:** withholding the name creates artificial suspense.

**Synthetic example:**  
Only one person had objected before launch. **Mara.**

### adjective-fragment — Adjective or predicate fragment

**Job:** Isolate a state, judgment, or quality after the referent is already unmistakable.

**Use when:** the compressed predicate gains force from omission.

**Avoid when:** the missing subject creates ambiguity or a fake dramatic beat.

**Synthetic example:**  
The prototype finally ran end to end. **Slow, but usable.**

### equative-dash-explanation — Equative setup plus explanatory dash

**Job:** Name the category or diagnosis, then reveal the concrete proposition that defines it.

**Use when:** the second half earns the abstract label rather than merely restating it.

**Avoid when:** the dash hides a weak relation or adds drama to an ordinary copular sentence.

**Synthetic example:**  
**The problem was simple—no one owned the handoff.**

### linking-verb-nominal-clause — Linking verb plus nominal/content clause

**Job:** Make a proposition itself the complement of a linking verb so the sentence can classify, define, or diagnose it.

**Use when:** the content clause is what the subject *is*, *means*, or *becomes*.

**Avoid when:** a direct verb would express the action more clearly.

**Synthetic example:**  
**The risk is that nobody knows which version is authoritative.**

### corrective-sentence — Corrective or replacement sentence

**Job:** Replace the reader's likely category with a better one.

**Use when:** the central movement is correction: not X, but Y; not merely X, rather Y.

**Avoid when:** the rejected framing was never plausible or present.

**Synthetic example:**  
**It was not a scheduling problem. It was an ownership problem.**

### aphoristic-relative-subject — Relative/content clause as aphoristic subject

**Job:** Turn a relation or selection into the grammatical subject of a compact general claim.

**Use when:** the abstraction is earned by observed particulars and the statement remains scoped.

**Avoid when:** the form produces a universal-sounding maxim unsupported by the material.

**Synthetic example:**  
**What survives compression is usually what the piece is really about.**

### cleft-focus — Cleft sentence for contrastive focus

**Job:** Put one constituent under explicit contrastive focus.

**Use when:** correcting which actor, object, time, cause, or quantity matters.

**Avoid when:** there is no real contrast or correction to mark.

**Synthetic example:**  
**It was the denominator, not the percentage, that had changed.**

### cumulative-right-branching — Cumulative right-branching sentence

**Job:** State the base assertion early, then accumulate qualifying, specifying, or perceptual material in the order the reader can absorb it.

**Use when:** the thought genuinely gathers detail or consequence after the clause has become grammatically complete.

**Avoid when:** additions are merely stacked because a long sentence feels sophisticated.

**Synthetic example:**  
**She opened the report, looking first at the missing rows, then at the timestamps, then at the one field no system had owned.**

### embedded-when-clause — Embedded temporal/conditional when-clause

**Job:** Locate the point at which a state, judgment, or consequence changes.

**Use when:** the temporal boundary is also a logical boundary.

**Avoid when:** `when` blurs cause and time or implies a repeatable rule the evidence does not establish.

**Synthetic example:**  
**The risk changed when the workaround became the workflow.**

### because-fragment — Deliberate causal fragment

**Job:** Isolate a cause after the consequence is already complete and available.

**Use when:** the causal relation is real, the antecedent is unambiguous, and the fragment changes emphasis or timing.

**Avoid when:** causality is uncertain, invented, or already obvious enough that the fragment merely overexplains.

**Synthetic example:**  
We stopped pretending the original plan still fit. **Because the deadline had moved.**

## Repetition and parallel structure

### repeated-prepositional-openers — Repeated opening prepositional phrases

**Job:** Hold the grammatical frame constant while the scene, stage, location, or condition changes.

**Use when:** recurrence itself reveals development or comparison.

**Avoid when:** repeated openings are only ornamental symmetry.

**Synthetic example:**  
**At the door, she hesitated. At the desk, she signed. At the elevator, she called home.**

### repeated-between-frame — Repeated “between X and Y” relational frame

**Job:** Compare multiple tensions or intervals through one stable relational frame.

**Use when:** each pair is genuinely comparable and the recurrence exposes structure.

**Avoid when:** the repeated frame falsely implies equivalence among unlike relations.

**Synthetic example:**  
**Between promise and proof, the claim weakened. Between proof and use, it changed again.**

### parallel-svo — Parallel subject–verb–object clauses

**Job:** Make genuinely parallel actions or responsibilities visible through repeated clause shape.

**Use when:** the actors or actions deserve comparable grammatical weight.

**Avoid when:** syntactic balance implies equal evidence, importance, causality, or completeness that the content does not support.

**Synthetic example:**  
**The team cut scope. The agent checked dependencies. The reviewer tested the claim.**

### escalating-verbs — Escalating verb sequence

**Job:** Use verb choice itself to show increase in force, consequence, commitment, or transformation.

**Use when:** the underlying events truly intensify.

**Avoid when:** stronger verbs exaggerate the evidence.

**Synthetic example:**  
**The delay annoyed the team, disrupted the launch, then canceled the test window.**

### anaphora-escalation — Anaphora with semantic escalation

**Job:** Repeat an opening while the object, consequence, or conceptual depth changes.

**Use when:** repetition creates a controlled ladder rather than redundant emphasis.

**Avoid when:** the repeated words are doing more work than the ideas.

**Synthetic example:**  
**We checked the data. We checked the rule. We checked the assumption that made both seem safe.**

## Spatial and conceptual framing

### metaphorical-prepositional-frame — Metaphorical prepositional frame

**Job:** Use a spatial relation to organize an abstract relation while keeping the metaphor coherent and limited.

**Use when:** the spatial frame clarifies hierarchy, concealment, pressure, boundary, or movement.

**Avoid when:** the metaphor collides with another active figurative world or substitutes image for explanation.

**Synthetic example:**  
**Under the polish sat the same unresolved assumption.**

### abstract-to-concrete-turn — Abstract claim followed by concrete image or mechanism

**Job:** State a general diagnosis, then earn it with a specific observable fact, mechanism, or scene.

**Use when:** the concrete second sentence supplies evidence, embodiment, or consequence.

**Avoid when:** the concrete detail is invented merely to make the prose vivid.

**Synthetic example:**  
**The process had no owner. Three approvals waited in three inboxes.**

## Choosing among forms

Do not ask, “Which construction would make this sentence more interesting?”

Ask:

- What relation is actually present?
- What should the reader know first?
- What deserves isolation or end position?
- Is the sentence correcting, accumulating, identifying, escalating, contrasting, defining, or revealing?
- Would a conspicuous form expose that movement more exactly than a plainer alternative?
- Does the form change the sentence's literal commitments?
- Does repetition represent a repeated idea, or merely imitate rhythm?
- Does fragmentation preserve enough omitted material for the reader to recover the thought?
- Does the form fit the writer's existing voice and the genre?

If the answer to the material-value question is no, use the plainer form.

## Composition principle

This repertoire exists to increase **available choices**, not to increase visible style.

The strongest use of the repertoire is often restraint: recognizing that a construction *could* be used and deciding the thought does not require it.
